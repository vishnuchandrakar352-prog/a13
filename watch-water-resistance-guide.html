Watch Water Resistance Ratings

The depth printed on a watch is one of the most misunderstood numbers in watchmaking. Here is what it actually means, and how to keep water where it belongs.

contents

  1. A pressure, not a depth
  2. Reading the common ratings
  3. Diver’s watches
  4. Seals age
  5. Simple habits

02.1

A pressure, not a depth

Water resistance ratings come from a pressure test in still water in a laboratory. Real life adds movement: a swimming stroke or a dive into a pool can briefly create far more pressure than the still-water figure suggests.

02.2

Reading the common ratings

A practical guide used by many makers:

  • 30 m: splashes and rain, not swimming.
  • 50 m: light swimming in calm water.
  • 100 m: swimming and snorkelling.
  • 200 m and above: diving, especially with a screw-down crown.

02.3

Diver’s watches

Watches labelled as diver’s watches are tested to a stricter international standard covering pressure, a one-way rotating bezel, legibility in the dark and resistance to salt water. A depth number alone does not make a watch a diver’s watch.

02.4

Seals age

Rubber gaskets around the case back, crystal and crown slowly harden. Heat, soap, perfume and sun cream all speed this up. Most makers suggest checking water resistance every year or two and replacing seals at each service.

02.5

Simple habits

Make sure a screw-down crown is fully closed before water. Never operate the crown or pushers underwater. Avoid hot showers and saunas, because heat expands parts and can let steam in. Rinse in fresh water after the sea.
